<p>In a recent pivotal case concerning immigration law, the Upper Tribunal has addressed the Secretary of State’s guidance on Zambrano rights, specifically scrutinizing the “realistic prospect” test applied to applications for leave to remain. The case of Mr Maisiri, a Zimbabwean national, has underscored critical issues regarding the assessment of Zambrano claims and the interplay with other immigration routes.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Background of the Case</h3><p>Mr. Maisiri, who had overstayed his visa since arriving in the UK in 2004, sought to regularize his status under the EU Settlement Scheme as a Zambrano carer. He argued that his British daughter would face severe detriment if he were deported. The Secretary of State, however, rejected his application, asserting that he might qualify for leave under alternative immigration routes, such as Appendix FM.</p><p>The First-tier Tribunal ruled in Mr Maisiri’s favour, critiquing the Secretary of State’s reliance on potential alternative routes rather than the immediate Zambrano claim. The Tribunal’s decision hinged on whether the focus should be on hypothetical scenarios or the facts of the case.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">The Secretary of State’s Appeal</h3><p>On appeal, the Secretary of State contended that the initial Tribunal had misinterpreted the legal relevance of applying under Appendix FM. The central argument was whether Mr Maisiri could avoid removal due to his strong ties to the UK, which might be considered under other immigration rules. The Secretary of State argued that a “realistic prospect” of securing leave through other routes should be a factor in assessing Zambrano claims.</p><p>This appeal was juxtaposed against the backdrop of a recent High Court judgment in <em>R (Akinsanya &amp; Aning-Adjei) v SSHD</em>, which questioned the validity of the “realistic prospect” test. The Secretary of State proposed delaying the decision pending a potential appeal against this High Court ruling, while Mr Maisiri’s legal team asserted that the High Court judgment directly undermined the Secretary of State’s position.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Upper Tribunal’s Analysis</h3><p>The Upper Tribunal, after reviewing written submissions, decided against further delay, finding the case straightforward enough to resolve without additional oral arguments. The Tribunal meticulously examined the interpretation of the Immigration Rules concerning Zambrano rights and noted the historical and legal context leading to the current situation.</p><p>The Tribunal found that the Secretary of State’s guidance—introducing a “realistic prospect” test—was flawed. This test required caseworkers to evaluate whether an applicant could obtain leave through alternative routes like Appendix FM, which the Tribunal found was not supported by the Immigration Rules or relevant case law. The Tribunal emphasized that decisions should be grounded in the facts of the applicant’s situation rather than speculative assessments of future immigration outcomes.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Legal and Policy Implications</h3><p>The Tribunal’s decision highlighted several key issues:</p><p><strong>1.  Inconsistency with Immigration Rules:</strong> The “realistic prospect” test, as advised by the Secretary of State, was found to be inconsistent with the Immigration Rules. The Tribunal underscored that the Rules focus on the present circumstances of the applicant rather than hypothetical possibilities.</p><p><strong>2.  Case Law and Guidance</strong>: The Tribunal reviewed relevant case law, notably the Court of Appeal decision in <em>Velaj v SSHD</em>, and concluded that the realistic prospect test did not align with established legal precedents. The guidance’s reliance on hypothetical scenarios was deemed both unworkable and unfair.</p><p><strong>3.  Impact of Recent Judgments</strong>: The Tribunal acknowledged Eyre J’s judgment in the related <em>Akinsanya &amp; Aning-Adjei</em> case, which critiqued the realistic prospect test and supported the view that Zambrano rights should not be denied based on potential eligibility for alternative routes.</p><p><strong>4.  Final Ruling: </strong>The Upper Tribunal upheld the First-tier Tribunal’s decision, affirming that the Secretary of State’s interpretation and guidance were incorrect. The Tribunal reinforced that the assessment of Zambrano claims should be based on the facts and not on speculative considerations regarding other immigration routes.</p><p>Upper Tribunal’s decision in <em>Maisiri (EUSS, Zambrano, ‘Realistic Prospect’ policy) [2024] UKUT 00235</em>, several key takeaways have emerged that significantly impact the understanding and application of Zambrano rights in the UK immigration system.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Key Takeaways</h3><ul class="wp-block-list"><li><strong>Discrepancy in Guidance: </strong>The Upper Tribunal has found that the Home Office’s “realistic prospect” test for assessing Zambrano claims is flawed. This test required decision-makers to evaluate whether an applicant might secure leave to remain under Appendix FM, which the Tribunal deemed inconsistent with the Immigration Rules. The Tribunal emphasized that the guidance’s requirement to consider hypothetical future applications under different immigration routes does not align with the actual legal provisions governing Zambrano’s rights.</li>
<li><strong>Focus on Actual Circumstances:</strong> The Tribunal stressed that the proper approach should focus on the current facts of the applicant’s situation, not speculative assessments of potential alternative routes. This aligns with the principle that the determination of Zambrano rights should be based on the immediate reality of the applicant’s circumstances rather than hypothetical future possibilities.</li>
<li><strong>Legal Precedents and Fairness</strong>: The Tribunal noted that the “realistic prospect” test was not supported by relevant case law, including the Court of Appeal decision in <em>Velaj v SSHD</em>. It pointed out that the test was not only legally unsupported but also potentially unfair and unworkable. This assessment was reinforced by the closure of the Zambrano route to new applicants as of August 2023, highlighting that relying on this test could lead to erroneous and unjust outcomes for individuals who are otherwise entitled to Zambrano rights.</li>
<li><strong>Impact of Recent Judgments: </strong>The decision also reflects alignment with recent High Court judgments, notably the Akinsanya case, which similarly critiqued the “realistic prospect” test. The Upper Tribunal acknowledged these judgments and found no reason to diverge from their conclusions on this issue. The consistency in judicial reasoning underscores the importance of adhering to the correct interpretation of the law.</li>
<li><strong>Practical Considerations</strong>: Finally, the Tribunal suggested that if a Zambrano carer’s appeal is dismissed based on the potential for obtaining leave under Appendix FM, it would be prudent for judges to consider alternative actions. This includes possibly staying the appeal to allow the appellant to pursue an application under Appendix FM, thereby preserving their rights as a Zambrano carer and avoiding unnecessary delays in case resolution.</li></ul><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Conclusion</h3><p>The case of Mr. Maisiri underscores a critical juncture in immigration law, particularly regarding the application of Zambrano rights. The Upper Tribunal’s ruling provides a clear directive that the evaluation of Zambrano claims must focus on the immediate facts of the case, rather than speculative prospects of alternative immigration routes. This decision rectifies previous misapplications of the guidance and aligns with a more principled interpretation of immigration rules and case law. For practitioners and policymakers, the case highlights the importance of adhering to the factual basis of claims and the need for precise guidance that reflects the true legal framework. Overall, the Upper Tribunal’s decision reaffirms that assessments of Zambrano rights must be rooted in the facts and legal framework rather than speculative prospects of alternative immigration routes. This ruling is a significant development in ensuring fairness and consistency in the application of immigration rules for non-EU carers of British citizens.</p>
