<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Facts:</h2><p>An Albanian national, trafficked and forced to sell drugs in Albania, sought asylum in the UK in 2018. The Home Office accepted his trafficking claim in July 2021. Despite a provisional decision on discretionary leave in November 2021, the Home Office refused to grant discretionary leave. The actual decision to refuse leave was not communicated to the claimant or his lawyer until a late stage in the judicial review.</p><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Legal basis of the discretionary leave for modern slavery victims</h2><p>Article 14 The Council of Europe Convention on Action against Trafficking in Human (ECAT) states:</p><p>The Council of Europe Convention on Action against Trafficking in Human Beings 2005, or “<a href="https://rm.coe.int/168008371d">ECAT</a>” as it is known and to which the UK is a signatory, provides for the following:</p><p>Article 14 – Residence permit 1. Each Party shall issue a renewable residence permit to victims, in one or other of the two following situations or in both: a. the competent authority considers that their stay is necessary owing to their situation; b. the competent authority considers that their stay is necessary for their cooperation with the competent authorities in the investigation or criminal proceedings.</p><p>‘Discretionary leave consideration for victims of modern slavery’. This said that leave would be granted in three situations, namely where:</p><ul class="wp-block-list"><li>leave is necessary owing to personal circumstances</li>
<li>leave is necessary to pursue compensation</li>
<li>victims are helping police with their enquiries</li></ul><h2 class="wp-block-heading">HC judgement:</h2><p>In the KTT case, the High Court found the guidance unlawful for not granting leave to remain to those with pending asylum claims fearing re-trafficking. The ruling emphasised that confirmed victims of modern slavery seeking asylum must be given leave to remain, as their stay is necessary for pursuing their asylum claims. The Court of Appeal agreed, stating there’s no basis for not interpreting Article 14.1(a) in its natural sense.</p><p>The High Court judgement has 4 crucial aspects:</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Secret Policy:</h3><p>Mr Justice Lane held that the claimant’s complaint centres on the accusation of a “secret policy.” The court emphasises that the SSHD had the authority to amend or revoke the ECAT policy. It suggests that the SSHD could have publicly announced an amendment, stating a general decision-making pause for individuals covered by the KTT declaration or a broader category. While acknowledging potential political consequences, the court stresses that such considerations should not compromise the rule of law principles, emphasising that political reasons cannot excuse illegal actions.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Breach of Article 8</h3><p>Mr Justice Lane held “I therefore find that Article 8(1) is engaged in the claimant’s case. I must, accordingly, consider the period that the SSHD’s unlawful interference with the claimant’s Article 8 rights lasted….. I therefore conclude that there was a substantive breach of Article 8 ECHR from 8 December 2021 to 18 January 2023.”</p><p>In reaching this verdict he took into account R (Balajigari) v Secretary of State for the Home Department <a href="https://www.bailii.org/cgi-bin/redirect.cgi?path=/ew/cases/EWCA/Civ/2019/673.html">[2019] 1 WLR 4647</a> where in paragraph 81 of the judgment of Underhill LJ, where the Balajigari claimants pointed to the legal consequences of remaining in the United Kingdom without leave; namely, those stemming from the so-called “hostile environment”, which included severe restrictions on the right to work, rent accommodation, have a bank account and hold a driving licence. In paragraph 91, Underhill LJ did not conclude whether those consequences were such as to engage Article 8, he nevertheless observed that it was “not difficult to see that in some cases some of the legal consequences of being present in the UK without leave – for example, the inhibitions on renting accommodation – may engage Article 8; but their impact will vary from case to case…”.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Breach of ARTICLE 14</h3><p>Mr Justice Lane agreed with the claimant’s argument that two groups should be compared: victims of modern slavery seeking asylum and victims of modern slavery not seeking asylum. The Home Secretary’s differential treatment, providing a decision on leave for the latter group but not for the former, was deemed unjustified. Both groups were considered to have an equal need for leave, leading to the conclusion that there was also a violation of Article 14.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">DUTY OF CANDOUR, PRIVILEGE AND IRRELEVANCE</h3><p>Mr Justice Lane observed that the claimant accuses the SSHD of breaching the duty of candour by not disclosing an unpublished policy and decisions regarding the claimant’s entitlement to modern slavery leave in a pre-action response. The SSHD’s overall approach to the duty of candour was criticised for revealing as little as possible and responding only to specific requests. The SSHD’s extensive redactions, especially regarding Option A-D, were deemed unjustified, leading to additional effort and expenses for the claimant. The SSHD misunderstood the qualification for redaction based on irrelevance. The SSHD’s reluctance to provide information on draft decisions was questioned, with a note that it was relevant to the claimant’s case. Swift J’s guidance from a different case suggested that disclosing parties should explain redactions at the point of disclosure, preferably in a witness statement. The SSHD’s failure to follow these principles, including the error in redaction, was considered a serious breach of the duty of candour.</p><p>Mr Justice Lane concluded that The SSHD’s approach to disclosure, redactions, and explanations was criticised for hindering a fair and proportionate legal process. The claimant’s legal team faced challenges in obtaining necessary information, impacting the efficiency of the proceedings. Overall, the SSHD’s actions were seen as falling short of the required standards for candour and transparency in legal proceedings.</p><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Supreme Court:</h2><p>On 28 October 2022, the Supreme Court refused the Home Secretary permission to appeal the Court of Appeal’s decision in KTT.</p><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Response</h3><p><a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/granting-discretionary-leave">New guidance</a> was published on 16 March 2023 providing for discretionary leave to be granted in line with KTT to those who had a positive conclusive grounds decision made before 30 January 2023 and who had an outstanding asylum claim based “in a material part” on the risk of re-trafficking.</p><p>In summary, XY v Secretary of State for the Home Department [2024] EWHC 81 (Admin) sheds light on critical deficiencies in the treatment of modern slavery victims within the UK asylum system. The judgment, fortified by the Supreme Court’s denial of appeal, underscores the gravity of procedural lapses and breaches of fundamental rights.</p><p>As a legal professional, I emphasise the importance of upholding the rule of law, free from political influences. The case’s impact extends beyond XY, signalling a clarion call for transparency and adherence to legal principles in immigration policies.</p>
